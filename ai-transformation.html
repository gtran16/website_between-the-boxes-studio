AI transformation as a culture shift

How a design department of up to 150 people learned AI together, through listening, peer-led learning, and real problems, in a program that kept running after I left.

my role, in between

As executive sponsor, I sat between our VP and the program team, and between the people excited about AI and the people worried about it. My job was to hold the direction, turn leadership questions into real challenges, and give a small team (a program manager, a researcher, a content designer, and early on a transformation consultant) room to run.

Strategize

What was the problem?

AI arrived in everyone's work at once: exciting, overwhelming, and unevenly understood. People were experimenting alone, the department wasn't learning together, and some people wanted nothing to do with AI.

Everyone had a tool. Nobody had a shared way to learn it.

Strategize

Where did you start?

By listening. Leadership interviews came first, then a skills and attitudes survey that half the department answered. Most people were curious and optimistic, and they were clear about what they wanted: hands-on learning from each other.

With our VP, I set a North Star: reimagine how we work through human insight, shared data, and intelligent systems. The deeper goal came from a friend's program at another company: help everyone find their superpower, so people could shape their future roles themselves.

Four pillars

Human-centered

Start with a real problem, then decide what role AI should play.

The right tool

AI is one tool of many. Sometimes a simple rule works better.

Data literacy

Know what AI learns from, and how to tell if it's doing a good job.

Solve once for many

Shared problems get shared, documented solutions.

Build

What did you actually build?

The program moved in three steps, each one answering what the survey asked for.

Listen: interviews and a survey
→
Share: an AI expo where colleagues taught real use cases
→
Solve: challenge teams on problems leadership cared about

Between expos, a monthly newsletter, a podcast, and open collaboration hours kept people learning. For the second expo I added challenge teams: four to six people across disciplines, working on real problems like time-to-value and product-led growth.

I gave the program team a lot of creative freedom. My job was strategy: setting direction with our VP, then giving quick guidance and checking the work still served the goals.

Build

What about people who didn't want AI?

Some people objected over intellectual property, the environment, or what AI does to how we think. We made room for them: talks on those concerns, including one by our researcher on bias in AI, and a welcome for novel projects with no AI at all.

Understanding AI protects you, whether or not you choose to use it.

The case we made to everyone

Every challenge team also had to answer one question, so AI never became the default:

"Where was rules-based automation better than AI?"

We celebrated automation wins as much as AI ones. Bold experiments were encouraged, and every one had to show its reasoning.

Sustain

Did it stick?

In seven months, the expo grew from a first event of 19 sessions into a two-day event with 50+ contributors across 10 time zones, nearly 14 hours of recorded content, challenge teams, and a gallery of 15 creative projects.

Challenge team work fed directly into product-led growth, time-to-value, and AI assistant workstreams. Leaders began connecting their own people across teams, without anyone asking. Our program manager now runs the program, and the team started planning the next expo the week I left.

Reflect

What would you do differently?

I'd lock in how we'd measure change from the first month. We never ran the follow-up survey before I left, so the clearest evidence is participation. Next time, the before-and-after measures are part of the plan from day one.

How I used AI

I used AI the way we asked the department to: as a thinking partner, with me as the author. It helped me tighten my all-hands talk and the expo invitations. The North Star, the pillars, and the calls on direction came from conversations with people.
